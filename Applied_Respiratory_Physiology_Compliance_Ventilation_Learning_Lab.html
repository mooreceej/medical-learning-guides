Concept guide

The “condition/drug guide” adapted to this physiology lecture.

Slide map: compliance/elastance 5–6 · Laplace 7 · surfactant 8 · dead space/tidal volume 10–11 · Fick 12 · minute ventilation 13 · alveolar ventilation 14–17.
Mechanics

Compliance

Definition: distensibility; how much volume changes for a pressure change.

Core rule: more elastance/stiffness → less compliance.

Professor emphasis: compliance differs with initial inflation and between inspiration vs expiration.

Trap: “more elastic” does not mean “easier to stretch” here; stronger recoil means stiffer.

Mechanics

Hysteresis

Definition: inspiratory and expiratory P–V paths are different.

Mechanism: surface-tension effects at the air–liquid interface make inflation require more pressure.

Experiment: saline filling reduces the difference by removing the gas–liquid interface.

Mechanics

Laplace

P = 2T / r

Small r: higher collapsing P if T is unchanged.

Problem: without a stabilizer, smaller alveoli would tend to empty into larger ones.

Solution: surfactant lowers surface tension.

Mechanics

Surfactant

Source: alveolar type II cells.

Major lipid named: DPPC.

Action: lowers surface tension and helps prevent collapse of smaller alveoli.

Deficiency pattern: less volume achieved for a given expanding pressure.

Volumes

Dead space

Anatomic: conducting-zone air that does not reach exchange surfaces.

Physiologic: all tidal volume not participating in gas exchange.

Healthy lung: physiologic ≈ anatomic because alveolar dead space is small.

Lecture value: about 150 mL in examples.

Diffusion

Fick’s law

Gas transfer rises with the driving partial-pressure gradient and effective surface area, and falls as diffusion distance increases.

Direction rule: gradient ↑ → diffusion ↑; distance ↑ → diffusion ↓.

Ventilation

Minute ventilation

V̇E = VT × f

Total air entering both lungs each minute. It counts dead-space air too.

Example: 0.5 L × 16/min = 8 L/min.

Ventilation

Alveolar ventilation

V̇A = (VT − VD) × f

Fresh air reaching alveoli per minute after subtracting dead space.

Example: (500 − 150) mL × 16 = 5.6 L/min.

Applied

Apparatus dead space

A snorkel adds volume that is ventilated but does not add fresh alveolar gas on each breath.

Lecture example: added 150 mL snorkel + 150 mL anatomic dead space → total VD 300 mL; V̇A falls to 3.2 L/min at VT 500 and f 16.

Figure challenge 1

Prompt: One loop is from an air-filled lung and one from a saline-filled lung. Which loop should show the larger inspiratory–expiratory separation, and what mechanism explains the difference?

Answer: The air-filled lung shows the much larger loop separation (hysteresis). The gas–liquid interface creates surface tension, so more pressure is required during inflation. Saline removes that interface and makes inspiration and expiration more similar.

What to notice: compliance is the slope (ΔV/ΔP), and it changes across the curve. The low-volume beginning of inspiration is relatively flat—opening the lung initially costs pressure.

Exam trap: “saline-filled” is not more physiologic; it is an experiment used to isolate the contribution of surface tension.

Source: Respiratory_lecture_1_applied_physiology_POST.pptx — slide 6.

Figure challenge 2

Prompt: Rank the three curves from easiest to inflate to hardest to inflate. Then explain what this tells you about surface tension.

Answer: Saline-filled is easiest to inflate, normal air-filled is intermediate, and surfactant-deficient is hardest. Removing the air–liquid interface removes much of the surface-tension burden; removing surfactant does the opposite.

What to notice: at the same pressure, a more compliant lung achieves more volume.

Exam trap: surfactant is not simply “extra elasticity.” It lowers surface tension.

Source: Respiratory_lecture_1_applied_physiology_POST.pptx — slide 8.

Figure challenge 3

Prompt: V̇E and VD are constant across all three rows. Without seeing the right-hand column, calculate V̇A for each row and predict the ranking.

Answer: 6.8 L/min → 5.6 L/min → 3.2 L/min as VT falls from 1000 → 500 → 250 mL. Rapid shallow breathing wastes a larger fraction of each breath in the fixed 150 mL dead space.

Exam trap: equal minute ventilation does not mean equal alveolar ventilation.

Source: Respiratory_lecture_1_applied_physiology_POST.pptx — slide 15.

Applied Respiratory Physiology — Final Review

Mechanics

  • Compliance = distensibility. ↑ elastance/stiffness → ↓ compliance.
  • Lung recoils inward; chest wall recoils outward.
  • Hysteresis: more pressure to inflate than deflate an air-filled lung.
  • Baseline inflation keeps alveoli partly open and makes subsequent inflation easier.

Laplace + surfactant

  • P = 2T/r. Smaller r at same T → higher collapsing P.
  • Surfactant: type II cells; know DPPC.
  • Surfactant ↓ surface tension → ↓ collapse tendency.
  • Surfactant deficiency → lower volume at same pressure.

Volumes + dead space

  • Per breath: VT = volume reaching alveoli + VD.
  • Anatomic VD = conducting-zone volume; lecture example ≈ 150 mL.
  • Physiologic VD = all tidal ventilation not participating in exchange.
  • Healthy lung: physiologic VD ≈ anatomic VD.

Fick

  • Gradient ↑ → diffusion ↑.
  • Surface area ↑ → diffusion ↑.
  • Diffusion distance ↑ → diffusion ↓.
  • Think direction first; then attach the pathology.

Ventilation equations

V̇E = VT × f

V̇A = (VT − VD) × f

  • 500 mL × 16 = 8.0 L/min V̇E.
  • (500 − 150) × 16 = 5.6 L/min V̇A.

Common traps

  • Do not subtract VD when asked for V̇E.
  • Do subtract VD before multiplying by f for V̇A.
  • At equal V̇E, deep/slow breathing gives more V̇A than rapid/shallow breathing.
  • A snorkel adds dead space; it does not magically change baseline VT.
  • Equal total ventilation ≠ equal effective alveolar ventilation.
Lecture clarification flag: for dead space, preserve the lecture’s broad wording for course testing; the supplemental note below separates classic high-V/Q alveolar dead space from diffusion-distance problems.

Supplemental clarification

Dead-space nuance: the lecture uses a broad “physiologic dead space” framing that includes alveolar units failing to exchange gas and mentions diffusion-membrane dysfunction. The classic alveolar dead-space concept is especially tied to alveoli that are ventilated but inadequately perfused (high V/Q). For your lecture exam, preserve the professor’s wording; conceptually, keep “dead space = wasted ventilation” separate from “diffusion distance = slower transfer across an exchange membrane.”
