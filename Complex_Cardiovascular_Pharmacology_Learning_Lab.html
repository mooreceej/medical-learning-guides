2. Mechanisms

Atrial fibrillation: from trigger to complication

Pulmonary-vein/atrial ectopy→Chaotic atrial activation→No coordinated atrial contraction→Variable AV-node penetration→Irregularly irregular ventricular rhythm
Loss of atrial kick

↓ ventricular filling → ↓ stroke volume, especially when the ventricle is stiff → dyspnea/exercise intolerance.

Blood stasis

Especially in the left atrium/appendage → thrombus → embolic stroke risk.

Persistent tachycardia

↑ workload → LV dysfunction → tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy.

The reinforcing loop

AF with RVR→Persistent tachycardia→LV dysfunction→↑ filling pressures / LA enlargement→More AF substrate

Exam move: when EF is reduced, ask whether the structural disease caused the AF, the AF caused the structural disease, or both are reinforcing each other.

Stable angina: why exertion hurts

Fixed epicardial stenosis→Downstream arterioles dilate at rest→Exercise raises HR/contractility/BP→No further vasodilatory reserve→Supply-demand mismatch→Angina + ischemic ST changes

Rest works because it lowers myocardial oxygen demand back below the fixed supply limit.

LDL lowering: plaque biology

↓ hepatic cholesterol synthesis or ↑ LDL-receptor availability→↓ circulating LDL→Less lipid delivery to plaque→Plaque stabilization

The lecturer emphasized aggressive LDL lowering and noted that non-calcified (“soft”) plaque may regress in some patients when LDL is driven very low. Calcified plaque is much less reversible.

3. Condition & drug guide

Atrial fibrillation with RVR

Recognition: irregularly irregular rhythm, absent distinct P waves, tachycardia.

Look for drivers: structural disease/LA enlargement, hypertension, ischemia, thyroid disease, pulmonary disease/hypoxia, infection, alcohol/toxins, electrolytes, surgery/trauma.

Complications: embolic stroke, heart failure/pulmonary edema, tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy.

Decision: unstable → urgent cardioversion. Stable → rate/rhythm strategy + anticoagulation assessment.

Stable coronary angina

Recognition: predictable exertional symptoms, reproducible threshold, relief with rest.

Mechanism: fixed stenosis + limited coronary flow reserve.

Goals: relieve pain, lower myocardial O₂ demand, prevent disease progression and future MI.

Base strategy: risk-factor modification + statin + aspirin in established CAD; beta blocker for angina; add antianginals as needed.

4. Compare

Master “don’t mix these up” table

PairBest distinction
Stable vs unstable anginaStable = predictable exertion with fixed plaque; unstable = new/worsening/rest pain, usually plaque rupture + thrombus.
Unstable angina vs NSTEMIBoth can be ACS; NSTEMI has biomarker evidence of myocardial necrosis.
DHP vs non-DHP CCBDHP (nifedipine) = vascular; non-DHP (diltiazem/verapamil) = more cardiac conduction/contractility effects.
Rate vs rhythm controlRate = accept AF but control ventricular rate; rhythm = restore/maintain NSR with cardioversion, drugs, or ablation.
Sotalol vs amiodaroneSotalol: QT/torsades + reverse-use dependence. Amiodarone: lung/thyroid/liver toxicity, but usable in structural disease.

6. Lecture figures

Figure challenge A

Question: An older trial comparing two AF strategies showed little overall mortality advantage despite a physiologic appeal to sinus rhythm. What design/therapy limitations did the professor emphasize?

AFFIRM: the lecture emphasized intention-to-treat crossover, older/less clean antiarrhythmic drugs, and the fact that many patients assigned to rhythm control did not actually remain in sinus rhythm. The professor’s punchline was that toxicity of older rhythm drugs diluted the potential benefit of successful rhythm control.

Source: Cardiovascular_Pharmacology.V2.docx, p. 3.

Figure challenge B

Question: Which modern AF strategy is associated with the lower cumulative incidence curve in this figure, and why did the lecturer say it changed the old “rate control is enough” mindset?

Early rhythm control is the lower curve. The lecture used EAST-AFNET 4 to support a modern preference for giving appropriate patients an early chance at sinus rhythm, with fewer cardiovascular events than usual care in the trial.

8. Exam sheet

AF: the 30-second chain

Irregularly irregular + absent P waves → AF. Ask: unstable? clot/stroke risk? structural cause? EF? rate vs rhythm? anticoagulation? Treat BP/LV dysfunction/other drivers.

SituationMove
Hemodynamic instability / refractory ischemiaUrgent synchronized cardioversion
Rate control, preserved EFβ-blocker or non-DHP CCB; digoxin in selected cases
Reduced EFFavor appropriate β-blocker; avoid diltiazem/verapamil due negative inotropy
Rhythm maintenanceAmiodarone; selected patients may use sotalol/dofetilide with monitored initiation
CardioversionThink anticoagulation + thrombus timing/TEE strategy

CAD/angina: the 30-second chain

Predictable exertional pain relieved by rest → stable angina. Fixed stenosis → limited coronary reserve → supply-demand mismatch.

GoalBest memory hook
Reduce myocardial O₂ demandβ-blocker first; lower HR/contractility/BP
Abort breakthrough painSublingual nitroglycerin; sit down; up to 3 doses 5 min apart in chronic stable pattern per lecture
Persistent symptomsLong-acting nitrate, selected CCB, ranolazine
VasospasmCCB first-line; nitrate can help
Prevent progressionSmoking cessation, exercise/diet, aspirin in established CAD, aggressive LDL lowering
Asymptomatic stable follow-upDo not reflexively order routine stress testing
Classic traps: diltiazem with low EF; flecainide with structural heart disease; sotalol/dofetilide without QT monitoring; low K⁺ with digoxin; nitrate + sildenafil; confusing unstable angina with NSTEMI; stenting stable disease just because a stenosis exists.
