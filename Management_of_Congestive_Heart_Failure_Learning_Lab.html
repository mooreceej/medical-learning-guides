🎙️ What Dr. Harris emphasized in the transcript

  • Physical exam is not optional: he calls it the “number one thing” in diagnosing HF and warns against ordering an echo before seeing the patient.
  • JVP is the intravascular-volume clue: the internal jugular is his “dipstick”; peripheral edema alone can occur even when intravascular volume is low.
  • Echo is the imaging mainstay: fast, noninvasive, and useful for valves, systolic function, and diastolic function. He separately calls cardiac MRI the single best modality for RV function.
  • HFpEF + AF can crash filling: tachycardia shortens diastolic filling time and AF removes atrial kick, which can sharply reduce stroke volume in a stiff ventricle.
  • “Rattle off” the four HFrEF pillars: ARNI/RAS inhibition + one of the 3 evidence-based beta blockers + MRA + SGLT2 inhibitor. Diuretics are adjunctive for congestion, not one of the four.
  • Treat the patient’s goal and barrier: he repeatedly uses diet, literacy, transportation, cost, family beliefs, and fragmented health systems to show why a technically correct prescription can still fail.
  • Do not over-diurese: lowering filling pressure helps until preload becomes too low, causing hypotension and renal injury.
  • Acute vs chronic therapy matters: an already-prescribed beta blocker is generally continued during decompensation if the patient is not in shock or requiring inotropy; do not newly start one during the acute phase.

🎙️ Practical treatment details from the transcript

TherapyTranscript detail to remember
Loop diureticsFurosemide is common PO/IV. Torsemide is better orally absorbed and is favored by Dr. Harris when gut congestion/right-sided HF makes furosemide absorption unreliable. Bumetanide is very potent and used for refractory cases.
Sequential nephron blockadeIV loop + metolazone can be synergistic when diuresis is inadequate because the drugs hit different nephron sites.
NitratesVenodilation lowers preload; avoid reflexively giving nitrates in a preload-dependent right-ventricular infarct, where they can worsen shock.
Milrinone / dobutamineMilrinone provides inotropy plus pulmonary vasodilation and is especially useful in selected right-sided failure; Dr. Harris notes hypotension when starting it and personally often starts dobutamine first before transitioning.

8. Exam sheet

Use this only after you have worked the mechanisms and practice. It is intentionally compact.

Classification

  • HFrEF ≤40%; HFmrEF 41–49%; HFpEF ≥50%
  • HFimpEF: baseline ≤40%, rise ≥10 points, later >40%
  • Stage: A risk, B structural/asymptomatic, C symptomatic, D refractory
  • NYHA: I none, II moderate exertion, III minimal exertion, IV rest

Mechanisms

  • HFrEF: ↓contractility → ↑ESV → ↓SV/EF → SNS/RAAS → remodeling + congestion
  • HFpEF: ↓relaxation/compliance → ↑filling pressure → LA/pulmonary backup despite preserved EF
  • AF hurts HFpEF: loss of atrial kick + shorter diastole
  • CO = SV × HR; EF = SV/EDV

Recognition

  • Left: dyspnea, orthopnea, PND, rales
  • Right: JVD, hepatomegaly/RUQ discomfort, edema
  • S3 → systolic/dilated clue; S4 → stiff/diastolic clue
  • Pulsus alternans → severe LV systolic dysfunction
  • Kerley lines → interstitial edema
  • BNP/NT-proBNP supports HF diagnosis

Treatment logic

  • 4 pillars HFrEF: ARNI/RASi + evidence β-blocker + MRA + SGLT2i
  • Congestion → diuretics; too much → hypotension
  • Venous dilation (nitrates) ↓preload; arteriolar (hydralazine) ↓afterload
  • Inotrope → Starling curve up; use selected acute low-output states
  • Do not newly start β-blocker in acute decompensated HF; if already taking it and not in shock/need for inotropy, transcript generally favors continuation
  • MRA → monitor K+; true hyperkalemia matters, but transcript warns not to withhold automatically for merely upper-normal K+
  • HFpEF lecture: MRA + SGLT2i + diuretics PRN; transcript stresses that rapid AF is especially poorly tolerated because it removes atrial kick and shortens filling time
  • Loop nuance: torsemide has better oral absorption when gut congestion limits furosemide; refractory diuresis may use loop + metolazone
  • Nitrate trap: avoid in a preload-dependent RV infarct/shock phenotype

Acute pulmonary edema

  • Lecture example: LVEDP >25 mmHg
  • Hypoxia, tachypnea, frothy sputum, rales/wheezing
  • Diuresis + oxygen/positive pressure + nitrates; inotrope only if low output

Devices / access

  • CRT: reduced EF + wide QRS, especially LBBB; lecture: EF <35%, QRS >130/150 ms
  • HF hospitalization rate for Black patients: lecture says ~2.5× White patients
  • JVP = professor’s “dipstick” for intravascular volume; edema alone is not proof of hypervolemia
  • Echo = imaging mainstay; CMR = professor’s best modality for RV function
  • LVAD requires adequate RV function; can be bridge-to-transplant or destination therapy
  • Adherence barriers: patient + condition + therapy + socioeconomic + health-system

Common traps

  • Preserved EF ≠ normal pressure or normal output
  • Diuretics improve congestion but are not one of the 4 HFrEF mortality pillars
  • Metoprolol succinate, not tartrate, is the lecture’s evidence-based HF formulation
  • SGLT2 adverse-effect pearl from transcript: recurrent UTI is the practical “star this” concern; also know euglycemic DKA and rare Fournier gangrene
  • Professor table contains one arithmetic inconsistency—compute SV/EF yourself

Discrepancy 1 — EF terminology

Slide 12 says “systolic dysfunction: LVEF reduced (≤50%),” while slide 5 uses modern phenotype cut points: HFrEF ≤40%, HFmrEF 41–49%, HFpEF ≥50%.

How this lab handles it: use slide 5’s explicit classification thresholds for phenotype questions. Treat slide 12 as a broad “reduced vs preserved” teaching statement rather than the formal HFrEF threshold.

Current 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA guidance and the lecture’s own classification slide support HFrEF ≤40%, HFmrEF 41–49%, HFpEF ≥50%.

Discrepancy 2 — S3 vs S4

Slide 7 pairs S3 with systolic dysfunction and S4 with diastolic dysfunction; slide 8 lists S4 among severe systolic-HF findings.

Exam-safe synthesis: S3 is the stronger classic clue for significant systolic LV dysfunction/dilation; S4 points to reduced compliance/diastolic dysfunction. Both can coexist in HF, so the rest of the phenotype matters.

External clarification: Merck Manual Professional notes S3 ± S4 is usual in significant systolic LV dysfunction, while S4 without S3 is usual in diastolic LV dysfunction.

Discrepancy 3 — hypothetical table arithmetic

The table’s HFrEF/no-remodeling column gives EDV 200 mL and ESV 150 mL, which mathematically yields SV 50 mL and EF 25%. The table instead prints SV 38 mL and CO 3800 mL/min at HR 100.

Preserved lecture wording: the original numbers remain documented, but the calculator uses the actual formulas. The table is explicitly labeled hypothetical, so the core concept—not the 38 mL number—is the teaching point.

Current-guidance clarification — HFpEF

Slide 27 lists MRA, SGLT2 inhibitors, and diuretics as needed. That is directionally correct but not a complete 2026 management framework.

Current ACC 2026 ECDP: HFpEF management now emphasizes early accurate diagnosis, SGLT2 inhibitors, MRAs including newer nonsteroidal options such as finerenone where appropriate, phenotype/comorbidity-directed ARNI/ARB/diuretics/beta-blockers, and cardiometabolic therapy (including obesity-directed strategies) when indicated.

Lecture-vs-current-practice caveat — morphine

Slide 26 lists morphine as a vasodilator/anxiolytic for acute pulmonary edema. This reflects older acute-HF teaching. Contemporary practice does not treat routine morphine as a core pulmonary-edema intervention; respiratory depression, hypotension, and observational harm signals have driven much more selective use.

For this course, know that morphine appears on the slide. Clinically, prioritize oxygen/positive pressure when needed, diuresis, and blood-pressure/hemodynamic-directed vasodilation rather than routine opioid use.

Adverse effects not fully supplied by lecture

The objectives explicitly require a major adverse effect/outcome for each drug type, but the slides only explicitly flag some (for example MRA hyperkalemia and avoiding beta-blocker initiation during acute HF). Therefore the drug guide adds standard adverse-effect reminders and labels them as supplemental.

Transcript-specific nuance — beta blockers in acute HF

The slide says “do not start if the patient is in acute heart failure.” The transcript adds an important distinction: new initiation is different from continuing a chronic beta blocker.

Professor’s practical rule: if the patient presents decompensated but is not in shock and does not need inotropic support, he generally does not stop the existing beta blocker. If shock/hemodynamic instability requires an inotrope such as dobutamine, the beta blocker is stopped.

Transcript-specific nuance — digoxin

Dr. Harris makes a strong spoken warning that needing chronic digitalis in the modern era is associated with worse outcomes. That wording is stronger than the classic randomized DIG-trial conclusion, which found reduced HF hospitalizations without an overall mortality benefit.

How this lab handles it: preserve the professor’s caution for course interpretation, but do not convert it into the blanket board rule “digoxin increases mortality in every HF patient.”

Transcript-specific nuance — devices

The transcript adds details not explicit on the device overview slides: LVAD support assumes the RV can deliver blood to the LV; HeartMate 3 is a continuous-flow centrifugal pump, so patients may have little/no palpable pulse; LVAD can be bridge-to-transplant or destination therapy. CRT places an LV lead via the coronary sinus to the lateral LV wall to restore coordinated contraction.

Transcript-specific nuance — patient goals & adherence

The professor repeatedly frames adherence as a clinical diagnosis: ask about literacy, diet/cooking skills, cost, transportation/pharmacy access, cultural/family beliefs, and fragmented care. He also emphasizes older/frail patients’ priority of maintaining independence and the need to discuss goals of care/hospice in truly end-stage disease.
