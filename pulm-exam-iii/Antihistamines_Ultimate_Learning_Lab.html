5. Recognition lab

Identify the receptor or drug from the clue pattern.

Pattern B

H2. Cimetidine/famotidine block this receptor.
Pattern C

Allergy drug + sedation + dry mouth + urinary retention + motion sickness benefit

Antihistamines — Exam Sheet

Receptors

H1: Gq → PLC → IP₃/DAG → ↑Ca²⁺. Airways/gut smooth muscle, endothelium, sensory nerves, CNS.
H2: Gs → AC → ↑cAMP/PKA. Stomach, heart, vascular SM, mast cells.
H3: Gi/o; presynaptic CNS brake on histamine + other transmitters.
H4: Gi/o; immune cells, chemo-attractive function.

Histamine effects

H1 → bronchoconstriction, epithelial secretions, pruritus/pain, endothelial changes, edema/urticaria.
H2 → ↑ gastric acid/pepsin; ↑ HR/contractility; vascular relaxation; mast-cell negative feedback.
Lewis: flush (NO/capillary dilation) → flare (axon reflex) → wheal (vascular leak).

1st vs 2nd gen H1

Diphenhydramine: lipid soluble, BBB, ~4–6 h → sedation + antimuscarinic/α/serotonin effects; useful in motion sickness via H1/M1.
Fexofenadine / loratadine: limited CNS exposure (lecture: P-gp), ~12–24 h → less sedation.

H2 blockers

Competitive H2 blockade → ↓ cAMP → ↓ H⁺/pepsin. GERD/ulcer/dyspepsia uses.
Cimetidine: CYP450 inhibition + androgen/estradiol effects → gynecomastia, impotence; galactorrhea noted.
Famotidine: safer class exemplar.

Other mechanisms

Cromolyn: mast-cell stabilizer; ↓Ca²⁺-dependent degranulation → prevention before release.
Pitolisant: block/inverse-agonize H3 autoreceptor → remove negative feedback → ↑ histamine → wakefulness; QT caution in lecture.

Big traps

Anaphylaxis: epinephrine is the emergency drug; antihistamines are not the acute rescue treatment.
Antimuscarinic: dry mouth, urinary retention, blurred vision—not excessive salivation.
Terfenadine history: CYP inhibition → high parent drug → hERG/IKr block → QT/TdP.
Autacoid: produced locally, acts locally, metabolized locally; massive release can become systemic.

Lecture-versus-reference clarifications

1) Hypersensitivity wording: The objectives/transcript emphasize Type I and Type III, and slide 7 labels Type III as complement-mediated with “contact dermatitis” as an example. Standard references classify allergic contact dermatitis as Type IV (T-cell–mediated, delayed); Type III is immune-complex/complement-mediated. For your lecture exam, know the professor's framing, but do not carry the contact-dermatitis example forward as standard immunology.
2) Ranitidine status is time-sensitive: The lecture uses ranitidine as a same-class comparison. FDA withdrew older ranitidine products in 2020 because of NDMA concerns, but FDA approved reformulated ranitidine tablets in November 2025. Thus, a blanket “ranitidine is withdrawn” statement is no longer current in 2026.
3) Slide 15 lists “anti-nausea – pyridoxine” under sedating-antihistamine clinical uses. Pyridoxine is vitamin B6, not an H1 antihistamine. FDA labeling for doxylamine/pyridoxine treats them as distinct components: doxylamine is the antihistamine; pyridoxine is a vitamin B6 analog. I preserved the lecture's anti-nausea emphasis but did not teach pyridoxine as an H1 blocker.

External verification used for these clarifications:
Merck Manual Professional, Contact Dermatitis (reviewed Apr 2025); Merck Manual Professional, Overview of Allergic and Atopic Disorders; FDA, “Approves Reformulated Ranitidine Following Comprehensive Safety Review” (Nov 24, 2025); FDA BONJESTA prescribing information (doxylamine + pyridoxine).
