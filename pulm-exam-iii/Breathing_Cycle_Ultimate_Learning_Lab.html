Professor-style trap: the largest lung volume does not mean alveolar pressure is still negative. At the end of inspiration, Palv has returned to atmospheric pressure and airflow is zero.

What the professor keeps coming back to

  • Quiet expiration is passive; forced expiration is muscular.
  • TPP = Palv − Ppl. The negative sign on pleural pressure matters.
  • Apex ≠ best ventilated. Apical alveoli are larger at rest but change less.
  • ΔTPP drives expansion. Base has the larger ΔTPP and greater compliance.
  • Obstructive = hard to empty. Restrictive = hard to expand.

Recognition lab

No calculations first—recognize the pattern from direction of change.

Pattern 1 · Pressure signs

Palv is slightly negative, Ppl is becoming more negative, and airflow is inward. Which phase?

Active inspiration. Thoracic expansion lowers both pressures; negative Palv relative to atmosphere pulls air in.

Pattern 2 · Turning point

Lung volume is maximal, Palv = 0, airflow = 0, Ppl is most negative. What moment?

End inspiration. The pressure gradient has disappeared, so flow stops before recoil begins.

Pattern 3 · Upright lung

Alveoli are larger at rest but show a smaller volume change with the next breath. Apex or base?

Apex. Apical units are already stretched and are less compliant.

Pattern 4 · Disease

Patient can pull air in but empties slowly; end-expiratory volume stays high. Obstructive or restrictive?

Obstructive. Expiration through narrowed/high-resistance airways is the limiting phase → air trapping.

High-yield traps

Quiet exhale ≠ muscle contraction. Forced exhale into ERV is muscular.
Apical alveoli are bigger but ventilate less per breath. Resting TPP and ΔTPP are different ideas.
At rest Palv is 0 everywhere. The vertical gradient is in Ppl, which changes regional TPP.
Obstructive patients can feel “I can’t breathe in,” but the lecture mechanism is inability to empty → trapped air.
