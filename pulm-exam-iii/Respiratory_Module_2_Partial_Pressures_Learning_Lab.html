2. Mechanisms

Normal physiology first, then change one variable and predict the consequence.

Slides 4–11 Transcript emphasis

Pressure staircase: why 160 → 150 → 100 mmHg?

Dry atmosphere
PO₂ = Patm × FiO₂
Sea level: 760×0.21≈160
→
Humidified airways
PIO₂ = FiO₂(Patm−47)
Water vapor occupies part of total pressure.
→
Alveoli
PAO₂ = PIO₂−PACO₂/R
CO₂ entering the alveolus further lowers O₂ partial pressure.
Mechanism, not a trick: each gas contributes to total pressure. Adding water vapor and alveolar CO₂ changes how much of that pressure can be attributed to O₂.

Ventilation ↔ CO₂: predict before reveal

If CO₂ production stays constant and alveolar ventilation doubles, predict PACO₂.

Lecture concept: PACO₂ ∝ V̇CO₂ / V̇A. You were told to understand this inverse relationship rather than memorize the full equation.

Then connect it to O₂: PACO₂ ↓ → the subtracted PACO₂/R term gets smaller → PAO₂ ↑.

Altitude: pressure problem → compensatory ventilation

Patm ↓
FiO₂ remains ~21%.
→
Atmospheric PO₂ ↓
Less O₂ pressure is available to inspire.
→
PIO₂ & PAO₂ ↓
Risk of tissue hypoxia rises.
→
Hyperventilation
Blows off CO₂.
→
PACO₂ ↓ → PAO₂ ↑
Compensation partially restores alveolar O₂ pressure.

Lecture side note: blowing off CO₂ can produce respiratory alkalosis. The professor explicitly framed the acid–base detail as “more to come,” not the main takeaway here.

Figure challenge A

Question: As alveolar ventilation increases, what happens to alveolar O₂ and CO₂ partial pressures, and why?

Interpretation: PAO₂ rises toward the inspired O₂ ceiling, while PACO₂ falls because ventilation clears CO₂. The two panels encode the same causal rule used in the alveolar gas equation.

Source after reveal: Respiratory_module_2_pressure_of_respiratory_gases_POST.pptx, slide 11.

Exam trap: saying “more ventilation brings in more O₂” is incomplete. The important linked mechanism is that more ventilation also lowers alveolar CO₂.

Figure challenge B

Question: What mechanism explains the first drop in PO₂, and what explains the second?

Interpretation: dry room air (~160) → humidified airways (~150) because water vapor contributes 47 mmHg; airways → alveoli (~100) because alveolar CO₂ adds a competing gas pressure represented by PACO₂/R.

8. Exam sheet

Compact final review. Use the button to print only this section.

Equations you need

Pgas = Patm × Fgas

PIO₂ = FiO₂(Patm − 47)

PAO₂ = PIO₂ − PACO₂/0.8

Professor instruction: ignore the correction factor; use R = 0.8.

Three anchor values at sea level

~160: dry atmospheric PO₂
~150: humidified PIO₂
~100: alveolar PAO₂

Water vapor: 47 mmHg
Normal PACO₂ assumption: 40 mmHg unless told otherwise.

Altitude logic

Patm ↓ → atmospheric PO₂ ↓ while FiO₂ stays ~21% → PIO₂ ↓ → PAO₂ ↓ → hypoxia stimulates hyperventilation → PACO₂ ↓ → PAO₂ partially rises.

Twofold hyperventilation: lecture model halves PACO₂ from 40 to 20.

Ventilation logic

At constant CO₂ production: V̇A ↑ → PACO₂ ↓. Through the alveolar gas equation, PACO₂ ↓ → PAO₂ ↑.

If CO₂ production ↑ and ventilation does not: hypercapnia.

Terminology

Hypoxia: low O₂ at tissue level.
Hypoxemia: lecture wording = low O₂ in arterial blood.
Anoxia: total O₂ deprivation at tissue level.
Hypercapnia: high arterial CO₂.
Hypocapnia: low arterial CO₂.

Common traps

1) Altitude changes Patm, not FiO₂.
2) Subtract 47 before multiplying by FiO₂.
3) Divide PACO₂ by 0.8 before subtracting.
4) Distinguish PO₂ in atmosphere vs PIO₂ vs PAO₂.
5) Higher FiO₂ can push PAO₂ above 150 mmHg.

One-line solve: Identify the compartment → identify what changed → predict direction → calculate → sanity-check against the causal chain.

Clarifications & adaptations

Supplemental clarification The lecture slide defines hypoxemia as “low O₂ content in arterial blood.” In standard clinical terminology, hypoxemia is more specifically a decrease in arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO₂), while arterial oxygen content (CaO₂) is a distinct quantity influenced heavily by hemoglobin and saturation. For your lecture exam, preserve the professor’s intended blood-vs-tissue distinction; clinically, keep PaO₂ and CaO₂ separate.
