1 · Start here

Build the diagnosis from the pattern

Repeatable reasoning approach

1. Identify the deposition context: plasma-cell/light chain, chronic inflammation, aging/transthyretin, dialysis, or inherited variant.

2. Map the organs: kidney, heart, nerves, GI/liver/spleen, skin/soft tissue, joints.

3. Look for a discriminator: periorbital purpura/macroglossia → AL; chronic inflammatory disease + nephrotic pattern → AA; older man + restrictive CM/CTS → ATTR; dialysis + arthropathy → Aβ2M.

4. Confirm amyloid: tissue → Congo red → apple-green birefringence → type the fibril protein.

5 · Recognition lab

Translate a finding into the underlying process

Finding

Periorbital purpura

Think: AL vascular deposition → friable vessels → red-cell extravasation after minor trauma/Valsalva.

Use it as a strong discriminator only in the right systemic context.

Finding

Macroglossia

Think: soft-tissue amyloid infiltration. In this lecture it is a classic AL clue, especially when paired with purpura, neuropathy, or plasma-cell disease.

Finding

Shoulder-pad sign

Think: visible anterior shoulder enlargement from glenohumeral fluid and/or amyloid infiltration of synovium/surrounding structures.

Finding

Thick wall + low voltage

Think: infiltrative wall thickening with impaired conduction—not true myocyte hypertrophy.

Finding

Nephrotic edema

Think: renal amyloid → proteinuria/nephrotic syndrome. Both AL and AA can do this; use the background disease to distinguish them.

Finding

Dialysis + chronic arthropathy

Think: Aβ2M deposition in synovium/cartilage/bone.

8 · Exam sheet

Amyloidosis Exam Sheet

Core map: precursor context → protein misfolding → β-pleated sheet fibrils → extracellular deposition → tissue-specific dysfunction → biopsy/Congo red/apple-green birefringence → treatment of precursor source.

Part I — Core framework & must-know content

Must know · Formation

Definition: disorder of protein folding with insoluble extracellular amyloid deposition.

Shared structure: β-pleated sheet.

Why damage occurs: deposits resist phagocytosis/proteolysis and disrupt organ architecture/function.

Severity depends on: rate, amount, and location of deposition; clinical manifestations also depend on precursor type and tissue distribution.

Must know · Four pathogenesis routes

1. High concentration of normal protein: β2-microglobulin in dialysis; inflammatory precursor in AA.

2. Weakly amyloidogenic normal protein over time: lecture gives β-protein/Alzheimer example; age-related ATTR concept fits prolonged susceptibility.

3. Acquired amyloidogenic protein: monoclonal Ig light chains → AL.

4. Inherited amyloidogenic variant: hereditary ATTR.

Organ manifestations: mechanism → clue

Kidney: glomerular/tissue deposition → proteinuria → nephrotic syndrome → edema. Common in AL + AA.

Heart: myocardial deposition → thick walls + stiffness → diastolic failure; conduction infiltration → arrhythmia/heart block → syncope. Systolic failure can occur later.

Nerves: peripheral + autonomic neuropathy → sensory loss, orthostasis/syncope, impotence, gut dysmotility; less common in AA.

GI/reticuloendothelial: hepatomegaly ± splenomegaly, GI bleeding, gastroparesis, constipation, bacterial overgrowth/malabsorption, pseudo-obstruction.

MSK/soft tissue: pseudohypertrophy, macroglossia, shoulder-pad sign, arthropathy.

Skin/hematologic: waxy thickening, easy bruising, plaques/nodules, periorbital purpura; bleeding can reflect vascular infiltration + ↓ factor X + liver dysfunction.

Diagnosis

Preferred tissue in lecture: abdominal subcutaneous fat-pad aspiration/biopsy.

Lecture performance: sensitivity 57–85%; specificity 92–100% for AL/AA.

Pathology: Congo red → apple-green birefringence under polarized light.

Then type it: immunohistochemical staining can characterize the amyloid fibril protein.

Cardiac pattern: increased wall/septal thickness + low voltage is a high-value mismatch. Arrhythmia/heart block may explain syncope.

Lecture slide 26: septal thickening >6 mm + “sparkling” echogenicity is labeled 60% sensitive / 100% specific.

Course note: remember the slide if directly tested. Clinical clarification: modern echo guidance treats granular speckling as nonspecific and uses a broader multimodality pattern.

Part II — Distinctions, stem decoder & traps

If the stem gives periorbital purpura + macroglossia + neuropathy/edema… identify a systemic infiltrative process → favor AL → look for a plasma-cell dyscrasia.
If the stem gives chronic RA/TB/AS + proteinuria/nephrotic syndrome… identify sustained inflammation → favor AA.
If the stem gives an older man + restrictive cardiomyopathy + bilateral CTS… favor age-related ATTR.
If the stem gives long-term dialysis + shoulder/joint disease… favor Aβ2M.
If pathology shows Congo red… the next recognition step is apple-green birefringence under polarized light.
If echo looks thick but ECG voltage is low… think infiltration, not ordinary myocyte hypertrophy.
Trap

Hematuria is not the professor's renal answer for amyloid; the lecture emphasizes proteinuria/nephrotic syndrome.

Do not use one organ alone to type amyloid—combine context + distribution + discriminating signs.

Nuance

AL and AA can both affect the kidneys and GI tract. What separates them is the precursor context and the presence/absence of AL-heavy features such as periorbital purpura, macroglossia, neuropathy, and broader cardiac/soft-tissue involvement.

Course vs clinical clarification

The lecture slide reports septal thickening >6 mm + myocardial sparkling echogenicity as 60% sensitive / 100% specific. For the course, keep that slide-specific statement available if asked directly.

Modern echocardiography guidance says granular/speckled myocardial appearance lacks specificity when used alone and cardiac amyloidosis is evaluated using a constellation of ECG, echo/strain, biomarkers, CMR/nuclear imaging, and amyloid typing.

Supplemental references: 2021 ESC cardiac amyloidosis position statement; 2023 British Society of Echocardiography guideline. These clarifications supplement rather than replace what the professor taught.

The lecture transcript also says a normal NT-proBNP excludes cardiac amyloidosis. Modern guidance instead uses disproportionately elevated NT-proBNP as a red flag/monitoring marker rather than treating a single normal value as an absolute standalone exclusion rule.
