5. Recognition lab

Short “name the decisive feature” drills. Reveal the answer only after you commit mentally.
Hospital pneumonia + Pseudomonas

Which detail narrows cefepime vs pip–tazo?

Anaerobic need. Pip–tazo covers anaerobes; cefepime does not.
MRSA + weekly CK

Which drug is being described?

Daptomycin. Then check the site: it is not a pneumonia drug.
Flushing during infusion

True vancomycin allergy or infusion reaction?

Infusion-related histamine release. Slow the infusion; antihistamine may help.
30S + cations + esophagus

What drug family?

Tetracyclines, especially doxycycline in this lecture.
High peak + long interval

What PK/PD strategy?

Aminoglycoside concentration-dependent killing.
Viral release blocked

Which influenza target?

Neuraminidase. Think oseltamivir, zanamivir, peramivir.

6. Lecture figures

Actual lecture figures. Each starts as an interpretation challenge; source details appear only after reveal.

Figure challenge A

Question: Which exposure metric on this graph belongs to beta-lactams, and what dosing logic follows?

T > MIC. Beta-lactam efficacy tracks time above MIC. The lecturer used this to explain why frequency/sustained exposure remains important when dose-adjusting.
Source after reveal: Slide 8, “Pharmacokinetic/Pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) Relationships.”

Figure challenge B

Question: Use the matrix to explain why a penicillin allergy does not automatically exclude every cephalosporin.

Cross-reactivity is tied to side-chain similarity, not the beta-lactam ring alone. The lecturer repeatedly singled out cefazolin as having a distinct side chain on this chart.
Source after reveal: Slide 16, beta-lactam side-chain cross-reactivity table. Severe SCAR histories are a separate high-risk scenario.

Figure challenge C

Question: Follow the influenza life cycle. At what stage do neuraminidase inhibitors act, and why does that make timing important?

Try to answer before revealing the labeled slide.
Release from the infected cell. Oseltamivir/zanamivir block neuraminidase, reducing spread to new cells. Early treatment interrupts more replication cycles.
Source after reveal: Slide 55, influenza replication cycle.

Figure challenge D

Question: What physical event is prevented in the lower panel, and which drug class does that correspond to?

Commit to an answer before revealing the labeled figure.
The inhibitor prevents virion release from the host-cell surface → neuraminidase inhibitor class.
Source after reveal: Slide 58.

Core anti-pseudomonal logic

Inpatient common anchors→ cefepime OR pip–tazo→ ask if anaerobes are also needed→ pip–tazo already covers them; cefepime does not

Lecture nuance: ertapenem does not cover Pseudomonas; meropenem does. Gentamicin is no longer treated as clinically anti-pseudomonal; tobramycin is the aminoglycoside association. Oral anti-pseudomonal options mentioned in the Q&A were ciprofloxacin/levofloxacin, but fluoroquinolones are formally deferred to another lecture.

Stem decoder

“Keep above MIC”Beta-lactam → T > MIC.
“Highest peak”Aminoglycoside/daptomycin → concentration dependent.
“AUC 400–600”Vancomycin.
“Pseudomonas + anaerobes”Pip–tazo; or build cefepime + anaerobic coverage.
“MRSA pneumonia”Vancomycin/linezolid; avoid daptomycin.
“Flushing during vanco infusion”Histamine infusion reaction → slow infusion.
“CK weekly”Daptomycin.
“30S mutation”Doxycycline/aminoglycoside family.
“Nec fasc toxin suppression”Clindamycin or linezolid.
“HyperK + PJP”TMP–SMX.
“Cystitis, no fever/flank pain”Nitrofurantoin or selected fosfomycin.
“Inhaled flu drug + bronchospasm”Zanamivir.
“COVID + oxygen requirement”Dexamethasone enters the plan.
“RSV prevention in infant”Maternal vaccine this pregnancy OR infant monoclonal antibody.
