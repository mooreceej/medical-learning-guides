Fast PFT rule

Obstruction ≠ “low FEV1” alone

Low FEV1/FVC is the defining pattern in the lecture. If FVC is also low, check lung volumes: high RV/TLC supports air trapping; low TLC supports restriction.

Fast pathology rule

Where is the lesion?

Chronic bronchitis = bronchi/airways with mucus-gland/goblet-cell hyperplasia. Emphysema = acinus/alveolar walls with destructive enlargement, recoil loss, and often lower DLCO.

Part I — Core framework & must-know content

Must know: COPD is a heterogeneous syndrome with persistent respiratory symptoms and persistent, often progressive airflow obstruction from airway disease (bronchitis/bronchiolitis) and/or alveolar destruction (emphysema). Symptoms and exacerbation burden do not have to match the severity of spirometric obstruction.

2) Core map

Exposure/host factor → chronic inflammation or antiprotease deficit → airway narrowing and/or alveolar-wall destruction → expiratory flow limitation → air trapping/hyperinflation → dyspnea/exercise limitation → exacerbation risk and systemic consequences.

5) Protease–antiprotease mechanism

Neutrophil proteases/elastase ↑ and/or α1-antitrypsin protection ↓ → alveolar-wall destruction. Smoking also produces oxidative stress that increases inflammatory protease burden and can reduce functional antiprotease activity. Congenital α1-antitrypsin deficiency removes antiprotease protection systemically.

6) Chronic bronchitis mechanism + definition

Smoking/irritant → airway hyperreactivity + goblet/mucous-gland hyperplasia → mucus hypersecretion + squamous metaplasia → impaired clearance → mucus plugs/recurrent infection → obstruction.

Formal test definition: productive cough for ≥3 consecutive months in ≥2 consecutive years.

8) Imaging recognition

CXR: hyperlucency, increased AP diameter/barrel chest, flattened diaphragm. CT: emphysema = low-attenuation black lucencies and reduced vascular markings; may also show mucus plugging/bronchial-wall thickening.

2) Transcript-only / professor-emphasis nuances

Nuance — bronchodilator testing: Acute FEV1 reversibility does not reliably predict whether a patient will feel better on bronchodilator therapy. Do not confuse diagnostic test response with treatment usefulness.
Nuance — dynamic hyperinflation: During exercise, faster breathing leaves less expiratory time. Obstructed lungs do not empty before the next breath → end-expiratory volume rises → diaphragm flattens → work of breathing rises. This can drive dyspnea even when oxygen is not the main problem.
Nuance — eosinophils: COPD can have an eosinophilic/type-2 phenotype that predicts exacerbation risk and response to ICS/biologics, but eosinophils are not the defining airway histology of chronic bronchitis.
Nuance — CT: Seeing emphysema on CT does not automatically establish COPD; diagnosis still requires the clinical/physiologic context.

3) Acute exacerbation

Acute worsening over days (up to ~14 days in lecture): dyspnea ↑, sputum volume ↑, sputum purulence ↑. Often triggered by viral/bacterial respiratory infection. Treatment commonly includes bronchodilators + systemic/oral corticosteroids ± antibiotics.

Trap: Pneumonia, pulmonary embolism and cardiac disease can produce an apparent “COPD exacerbation.” The lecturer specifically emphasized PE as underrecognized.

4) Evidence-based interventions the lecturer highlighted

Smoking cessation; oxygen for qualifying resting hypoxemia; influenza vaccination; noninvasive ventilation for exacerbations; lung-volume reduction surgery in selected patients; pulmonary rehabilitation/exercise; class-appropriate bronchodilators ± ICS; biologics in selected frequent eosinophilic exacerbators.

5) Question-stem decoder

If FEV1/FVC is low → identify obstruction first → then ask whether RV/TLC show trapping/hyperinflation and whether DLCO points toward emphysema.
If the stem gives “upper lobe + smoker” → localize to centriacinar emphysema → mechanism is alveolar-wall destruction/recoil loss.
If “lower lobe + young + liver disease” → identify α1AT deficiency → whole-acinus panacinar destruction.
If “productive cough 3 months × 2 years” → chronic bronchitis definition → expect mucous-gland/goblet-cell hyperplasia.
If “clubbing” appears in a COPD-looking stem → pause → COPD alone is less likely; check for bronchiectasis/lung cancer/other disease.
If eosinophils are high in a frequent exacerbator → phenotype matters → ICS and later biologic therapy become more attractive.
If resting SpO2 is 87% → qualifies by lecture threshold → LTOT >15 h/day improves survival.
If only exertional desaturation is present → do not automatically claim survival benefit from oxygen; the lecture trial did not show it.

6) Last-minute traps

Trap: Low FVC ≠ restriction unless TLC is low.
Trap: Panacinar is not “more central”; it means the whole acinus.
Trap: Chronic bronchitis is a clinical definition; emphysema is a pathologic/anatomic definition.
Trap: Oxygen treats hypoxemia; it does not directly fix expiratory flow obstruction or dynamic hyperinflation.
Trap: Pulmonary rehab improves function/symptoms but does not reverse alveolar destruction or stop progression by itself.

Objective-sheet typo: the objectives document contains “COPC” and “congestive obstructive pulmonary disease.” This guide preserves the intended course concept as COPD = chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, matching the lecture title, slides/transcript and GOLD terminology.

Smoking / α1AT wording: one transcript phrase can sound as though smoking creates inherited α1AT deficiency. The guide instead follows the lecture diagram’s intended distinction: smoking increases protease/oxidative burden and can reduce functional antiprotease activity, whereas congenital α1AT deficiency is a separate inherited cause of low antiprotease protection.
