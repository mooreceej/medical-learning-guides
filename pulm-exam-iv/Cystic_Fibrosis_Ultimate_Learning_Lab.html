Lecture timeline — only the parts worth carrying forward

1938 — Dorothy Andersen describes “cystic fibrosis of the pancreas,” linking severe malnutrition with pancreatic pathology.
1940s–1950s — bronchiectasis/infection treatment and nutrition enter care; heat-wave observations lead to the salty-sweat/electrolyte clue.
1950s onward — sweat chloride becomes the diagnostic anchor; physiotherapy and later mucolytics/PERT expand symptomatic care.
1989 — CFTR gene and F508del defect identified.
2010 — universal US newborn screening in the lecture timeline.
2010s–2020s — CFTR modulators move treatment upstream from symptoms to protein function, dramatically changing outcomes.
Professor nuance: CF is more prevalent in people of Northern European ancestry but is not unique to that group. The lecturer explicitly warns against using ancestry to exclude the diagnosis.

Part I — Core framework & must-know content

1) One master causal map

Autosomal-recessive CFTR dysfunction → abnormal chloride/bicarbonate handling → airway surface liquid dehydration → thick/tethered mucus + extracellular DNA → impaired mucociliary clearance → chronic colonization → neutrophilic inflammation → airway remodeling/bronchiectasis → obstructive FEV1 decline → exacerbations/end-stage disease.

2) Sweat duct vs airway — the direction matters

SiteNormal CFTR role emphasizedCF effectExam result
Sweat ductReabsorb chloride; sodium is reclaimed with itSalt cannot be reclaimedHigh sweat NaCl; heavy losses can cause hyponatremic/hypochloremic dehydration
Conducting airwayBalances epithelial ion movement against constitutive sodium absorptionRelative Na+ hyperabsorption → water follows out of ASLDehydrated ASL → thick mucus → poor clearance

3) Diagnosis

Suspicion comes from newborn screen, symptoms, meconium ileus, family history, or CFTR testing. The lecture’s confirmatory test is sweat chloride: <30 mmol/L normal, 30–60 indeterminate, >60 abnormal/CF. Meconium ileus is presented as a “assume CF until proven otherwise” neonatal clue.

4) Genetics

Autosomal recessive. Carrier frequency ~1:25 in the lecture’s US example; carrier × carrier gives 25% affected risk per pregnancy. F508del is the most common variant; ~85% of US patients carry at least one copy. CF is not exclusive to White/Northern European patients.

5) Pulmonary progression

CF is treated as a conducting-airway disease. Bronchiectasis = progressive airway dilation driven by inflammation/infection; dilation further worsens clearance. Exacerbations produce more cough/sputum, chest pain, malaise ± fever, weight loss, worse CFRD control, and often ↓FEV1. 10–25% fail to recover prior FEV1.

7) Infection ecology

CF airways do not simply return to a sterile baseline. Organisms can colonize lifelong. The lecture’s management sequence is prevent → eradicate on acquisition → suppress once colonized → intensify during exacerbation.

10) Advanced + extrapulmonary

End-stage lung disease becomes a transplant target around FEV1 <30%; referral may begin around 40–50%. Post-transplant: no normal cough reflex, microbiology still matters, and the rest of the patient still has CF. Extrapulmonary disease includes pancreatic insufficiency/nutrition, CFRD, sinusitis, GERD/DIOS, liver disease, vitamin D/bone disease, hearing loss, depression/anxiety, and infertility (men: absent/obstructed vas deferens; women: thick cervical mucus/subfertility).

Transcript-only or professor-emphasized nuances

  • Salty-sweat history: heat-wave CF infants presented with hyponatremic, hypochloremic dehydration.
  • Thick mucus is more complex than dehydration: eDNA + mucus tethering also matter.
  • Mild bronchiectasis may have some reversibility, but the lecturer believes there is a point of no return.
  • Airway-clearance method has no clear winner; consistency matters. Transcript: at least 2×/day well, 4×/day with exacerbation.
  • Tobramycin can work despite a high in-vitro MIC because inhalation produces very high airway concentrations.
  • Azithromycin: CFF recommendation discussed as >6 years + PsA colonization, but lecturer says their practice often uses it more broadly. For course questions, follow the stem and professor framing.
  • After lung transplant, the patient may need trained periodic cough because vagal sensation is not restored.
  • Highly effective modulators have changed fertility, transplant rates, and survival—but they are not a cure and not available to everyone.

Common traps

  • Sweat vs airway: sweat problem is failed salt reabsorption; airway problem is net salt/water absorption out of the lumen.
  • Meconium ileus: do not downplay it as generic constipation in a neonate.
  • PsA: first acquisition ≠ chronic colonization. Management changes by state.
  • FEV1 improvement ≠ slowed decline. Old symptomatic therapies can raise the level and reduce exacerbations without changing the slope.
  • Corrector ≠ potentiator. Elexacaftor/tezacaftor/lumacaftor fix processing; ivacaftor improves channel function.
  • Stop codon: if no protein is made, there is nothing for standard protein modulators to rescue.
