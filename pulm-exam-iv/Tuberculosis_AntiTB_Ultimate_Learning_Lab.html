1 • Start here

Build the whole TB story in one chain

Use this guide in passes. First learn the disease-state distinction, then testing and imaging, then RIPE pharmacology, then resistance. The professor questions strongly reward recognizing the discriminator rather than memorizing isolated lists.

Reasoning approach

  1. Exposure or disease? Is the stem about risk/screening or symptomatic illness?
  2. Latent or active? Symptoms + CXR are the fast separator.
  3. Which active pattern? Primary vs reactivation vs miliary vs extrapulmonary.
  4. What confirms it? TST/IGRA for exposure; sputum AFB/PCR/culture for active pulmonary disease.
  5. What must happen now? Airborne isolation if active pulmonary TB is suspected.
  6. Which drug clue? Map mechanism ↔ toxicity ↔ elimination ↔ interaction.
  7. Any resistance markers? KatG/inhA → INH, rpoB → RIF, gyrA → FQ.

Fast anchors

LTBI: positive exposure test, no symptoms, negative CXR, not infectious.

Reactivation: apical/upper-lobe disease ± cavity.

RIPE: 2 months → then RI 4 months for typical drug-susceptible disease.

Ethambutol: eye. INH: nerves + B6. PZA: uric acid. RIF: CYP interactions.

Professor-tested patterns

Acid-fast stain • TST interpretation • reactivation CXR • renal failure/ethambutol • rifampin interactions • which drugs stop after month 2.

Do not let “positive TB test” equal “active TB” in your head. That is one of the lecture’s central distinctions.

Part I — Core framework + must-know content

Core map
Airborne exposure → alveolar macrophage uptake → impaired intracellular killing → T-cell/macrophage response → granuloma/caseation → containment (LTBI) OR loss of containment → active TB → pulmonary / extrapulmonary / miliary patterns → isolate + microbiologic confirmation → multidrug therapy.

Latent vs active

SymptomsNoneOften cough >2 wk, fever, night sweats, weight loss, hemoptysis; can be subclinical

Progression + epidemiology

  • About 5–10% of people with LTBI progress to active TB overall; risk is highest in the first 2 years after exposure but reactivation can occur decades later.
  • Lecture estimate for HIV: about 10% per year progression risk.
  • Lecture numbers: roughly one quarter of the world has LTBI; ~10 million active cases/year; ~1.5 million deaths/year. Use these as course figures.

Microbiology + pathogenesis

  • Mycolic acids → lipid-rich, hydrophobic, hardy, slow-growing, acid-fast cell wall.
  • MTB enters by airborne droplet nuclei; N95 protection for suspected active pulmonary disease.
  • Alveolar macrophages ingest MTB; MTB interferes with phagosome–lysosome killing → intracellular persistence.
  • Cell-mediated immunity (T cells, dendritic cells, monocytes/macrophages) builds granulomas; caseous center becomes acidic/oxygen-poor.
  • MTBC includes M. tuberculosis, M. bovis, M. africanum, M. microti. M. bovis is inherently PZA-resistant in the lecture.

Targeted screening

Screen people at increased exposure risk and/or increased progression risk: TB contacts, immigrants from high-burden areas, HIV, immunosuppressants/TNF-α blockade, transplant candidates, dialysis, silicosis, homelessness, correctional settings, healthcare workers, and selected illicit-drug users.

Suspected active TB: action sequence

  1. Airborne isolation (N95; negative-pressure room inpatient when applicable).
  2. History: symptoms, risk factors, previous TB, prior TST/IGRA, BCG.
  3. CXR: apical infiltrates/cavities are classic for reactivation.
  4. Obtain 3 sputum specimens for AFB smear + culture, ≥8 h apart, with ≥1 first-morning sample per current slide deck.
  5. Use molecular MTB PCR, commonly with rifampin-resistance testing.
  6. Remember: smear is fast but not perfectly sensitive; culture is slow (lecture: often 14–21 d, can take longer).

RIPE: mechanisms + toxicities

Standard drug-susceptible active TB: 2 months RIPE + pyridoxine → 4 months RI + pyridoxine. Ethambutol can be stopped once susceptibility to R/I/PZA is established. Longer treatment may be needed for high-burden cavitary disease, meningitis, or osteomyelitis.

Part II — Distinctions, resistance, and exam application

Why multidrug therapy?

Large bacillary population → spontaneous resistant mutants already exist → monotherapy kills susceptible organisms → resistant subpopulation expands. Combination therapy also covers different metabolic niches.

Professor-question patterns already signaled

  • Recognize acid-fast stain.
  • Know TST can be positive at a small induration in a high-risk patient and can have false positives.
  • Recognize reactivation CXR.
  • Connect renal failure → ethambutol in the professor key.
  • Connect rifampin → drug interactions.
  • Know which drugs stop after the first 2 months: PZA + EMB.

Nuances / traps from the lecture materials

  • Current slide deck says sputum samples are collected ≥8 hours apart; the supplemental older transcript says 2 hours. For this course guide, use the current slide/current lecturer: 8 hours.
  • The current transcript briefly says TST injection “subcutaneous,” but the objective/slide correctly labels the Mantoux test intradermal; use intradermal.
  • Professor Question 4 keys ethambutol for renal dose adjustment. The slide table also lists PZA as having hepatic + renal elimination, so preserve the keyed association for this course while remembering EMB is the most clearly renal and its optic toxicity rises with renal insufficiency.
  • Course epidemiology numbers are lecture figures; this guide does not replace them with outside current estimates.
